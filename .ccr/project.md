# Project

## Purpose

Canvas Learning is a course workspace for students and instructors. Students join courses, write
and submit assignment responses, and see their scores, written feedback, and suggested study
activities. Instructors read submitted work, publish scores and feedback, see which students may
need a check-in, and review how their courses are going overall.

The project owner reports that real people use it today, but they work with sample information:
they are asked to enter fictional personal details, and the grades shown carry no academic
weight. It comes with one sample summer term: four courses (data structures, web development,
data science, and product design), four instructors who each teach one course, and 26 sample
students with existing enrollments and submitted work.

## How people use it and are affected

**Signing in.** People choose "Student" or "Instructor" and then pick a name from a list. There
is no password. Anyone using the same computer can open any listed account, including an
instructor's account, and act as that person. Only students can create new accounts. Instructor
accounts are fixed, and each instructor sees only the course assigned to them.

**Where information lives.** Everything people enter is kept only in the web browser on the
computer where it was typed. It is not sent to a central server or shared with other devices.
An instructor sees a student's submission only if both use the same browser on the same
computer. A "Restore workspace data" option, available to anyone signed in, erases every new
account, enrollment, draft, grade, and comment in that browser after a confirmation prompt. If
saved information cannot be read, the app quietly returns to the original sample records and
shows a notice.

**A fixed calendar.** The app treats the date as August 15, 2026, at midday, and that date never
moves forward. Deadlines and submission times appear on a single world-standard clock, not the
person's local time. Late work is described as accepted until September 5, 2026. Because the
date never advances, that cutoff is not reached in the current version, so ungraded work always
stays open.

**Creating a student account.** Only name and email are required, and the email must not already
be in use. Names may contain any characters, up to 120. Optional fields cover preferred name,
date of birth, phone, mailing address, and three demographic questions:

- Gender identity: woman, man, nonbinary, a self-description, or prefer not to answer.
- Ethnicity: Hispanic or Latino, not Hispanic or Latino, or prefer not to answer.
- Race: one or more of five listed categories, or prefer not to answer.

The demographic section is collapsed by default and tells people to use fictional information.
It says these answers do not affect course access, grades, support flags, or study suggestions,
and that instructors do not see them on the course roster. In the current version, no screen or
calculation uses them. The owner says they are meant for future reports that compare outcomes
across groups. That reporting is a plan and does not exist yet. Students can edit their profile
at any time. There is no option to delete a single account.

**Joining courses.** Students enroll themselves from a catalog. There is no approval step and no
class size limit. Joining partway through the term includes every assignment, even ones whose
deadlines have passed. Those count as zero until the student submits them, so a student who joins
late usually starts with a low current grade. The catalog states this before people enroll.

**Doing coursework.** Each assignment asks for a typed response of up to 20,000 characters. There
is no way to upload files or images. Drafts are saved as the student types and stay private until
submitted. A student may revise ungraded work as often as they like. Each new version replaces the
last one and records the new time, so revising after the deadline turns on-time work into late
work. Once a score is published, the response is locked. Late work has no automatic penalty, but
instructors see it marked "Late" while grading.

**Grading.** Only the instructor assigned to a course can grade it. They read the response, enter
any score from zero to the maximum, and may add a comment. Saving publishes it to the student right
away. There is no review step or scoring guide. Instructors can change a published score or
comment later.

**How grades are worked out.** A current grade is points earned divided by points possible. It
counts graded work and any past-due work with no response, which scores zero. Work not yet due and
work still waiting for a score are left out. Letter grades use fixed cut-offs: A at 90%, B at 80%,
C at 70%, D at 60%, and F below that. Screens describe these as progress grades, not final grades.
Neither instructors nor students can change these rules.

**Support flags.** When a student's current grade in a course falls below 70%, that enrollment
appears to the instructor as "needs a check-in". It comes with the number of missing assignments
and a suggested next step, such as offering office hours or making a plan for missing work. The
app calls this a prompt for a conversation, not a diagnosis or penalty. Students see their own
grade highlighted when it is below 70%, but they are not told that they have been flagged.
Instructors are told to follow up through their institution's usual channels. The app has no
messaging.

**Course analytics.** Instructors see course averages, how much past-due work has come in, the
spread of letter grades, average scores for on-time versus late work, and activity over time.
Students are also placed in "engagement" groups based on how much past-due work they have
submitted: high (80% or more), moderate (50% or more), or low (below that). Instructors see these
groups as counts and averages, not names. The app notes that submitting work is only a rough sign
of participation and that differences between groups do not prove cause.

**Study suggestions.** Each student's dashboard suggests up to three activities from a fixed list
and gives a pacing tip. Fixed rules make the choice. They place each course grade in a band:
below 70% means extra foundation work, 90% or more means stretch material, and the rest gets core
practice. Students with no grade yet are treated as being in the middle. The rules also use
keywords from the student's weakest or missing assignment and the student's lateness pattern. The
dashboard says these are not AI-generated and do not affect grades. A small companion program on
the same computer makes these suggestions and the instructor analytics. It receives only scores,
dates, and enrollments, never response text or feedback, and it keeps nothing. If it is not
running, the rest of the app still works and those panels show a warning.

## Choices, access, and correction

- **What people need:** someone must install and start the app on a computer. People then use it
  in a web browser on that computer. Students and instructors who want to share work must use the
  same browser on that computer.
- **Language and format:** the interface is in English only, and dates appear in United States
  format.
- **Accessibility:** form fields are labeled, errors are announced to screen readers, and
  important explanations sit behind small information buttons. How well the whole experience works
  for keyboard or screen-reader users has not been established here.
- **Who decides:** the instructor alone sets each score and comment. Fixed rules, which no user can
  change, set the grade calculation, letter cut-offs, the 70% support threshold, engagement groups,
  and study suggestions.
- **Seeing why:** information buttons explain how grades, flags, completion, and suggestions are
  worked out. Students see their score and the instructor's comment, but no breakdown of how the
  score was reached.
- **Questioning or correcting a result:** students cannot reopen graded work or dispute a score in
  the app. They are told to contact their instructor. No process for that contact or for appeals is
  established. Instructors can correct a published grade at any time.
- **Personal information:** profile details and coursework stay in the browser where they were
  entered. They are not protected from other people using the same computer. Instructors see
  enrolled students' names, emails, grades, and submitted text, but not demographic answers or
  contact details beyond email.
- **Effects:** because current use involves fictional details and grades with no academic weight,
  real academic consequences are not expected today. Late joiners' grades, flags shown only to
  instructors, wide-open account access, and possible future equity reporting are the areas most
  likely to matter if real records are used later.
